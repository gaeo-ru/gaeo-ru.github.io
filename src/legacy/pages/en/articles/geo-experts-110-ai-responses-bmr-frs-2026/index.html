GEO Experts in AI Answers: Findings from 110 Responses and Two IndexResearch Studies

Alexey YakovlevPublished: November 2, 2026Updated: November 2, 2026GEO / AEO / Research

Among 110 AI responses, Alexey Yakovlev was mentioned in 75 (BMR 68.2%) and appeared first among named people in 58 (FRS 52.7%). A transparent review of the methodology, competing results, and the limitations of an interested-party study.

In a sample collected on October 30, 2026, Alexey Yakovlev appeared in 75 of 110 AI responses (BMR 68.2%) and was the first named person in 58 (FRS 52.7%).

These figures come from two IndexResearch reports on the visibility of people associated with GEO and AEO services in Russia. I am examining them here on my own professional website, with a particular interest in how the visibility of my practice is measured. The distinction between appearing somewhere in an answer and being named first matters more than a headline ranking.

Who commissioned the research, and why disclosure matters

I am Alexey Yakovlev, the owner of GAEO.ru and a co-founder of IndexResearch. GAEO arranged and paid for the collection of responses through BrandFound. The topics of the questions were proposed by an interested party, and my own name is among those measured. IndexResearch is therefore not an organizationally independent evaluator of my professional performance.

The two public reports cover mention frequency (BMR) and the first person named (FRS). The BMR dataset and reproducibility code and the FRS algorithm and accompanying data are available on GitHub. The methodology was formally documented after the responses had been collected; this was not a preregistered independent study.

This page is my analysis of what the reports do and do not show. The complete list of 74 people, provider matrices and evidence identifiers remain in the original reports. I am not using this page to publish a new ranking of the most qualified GEO consultants.

What was actually measured

The collection took place on October 30, 2026, from 10:34 to 10:51 Moscow time. It contains 110 valid answers across 11 AI interfaces, with 10 questions per interface: ChatGPT, Perplexity, DeepSeek, Grok, Claude, Google Gemini, Google AI Mode, Google AI Overviews, GigaChat, Yandex Search with Alice, and the standalone Alice AI chat.

The Russian-language prompts asked for individual GEO/AEO specialists who could help a company appear in AI-generated answers. Some requested several names, others asked for one consultant, an independent practitioner who personally manages client projects, a full-service specialist, or someone with documented cases. My name was not included in the questions. Nevertheless, their subject matter and wording may have favored people with specific public positioning.

Across the responses, the researchers identified 74 distinct person names and 411 answer–person pairs. A single answer can name several people. The dataset has no recorded technical gaps.

There is a limitation to direct cross-platform comparisons. For ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, DeepSeek and Grok, six prompts included an additional explicit reference to “in Russia.” The other six interfaces received versions without that addition. Some apparent differences between platforms may thus reflect differences in prompt wording, not the behavior of the AI products alone. The sample also includes only one recorded answer per prompt–interface pair.

BMR: presence anywhere in the response

In this study, Brand Mention Rate (BMR) means the percentage of answers in which the researchers detected a recognizable full person name. Multiple repetitions in a single response count once. The denominator for each person in the full dataset is 110.

Formula: BMR = answers containing the name / 110 × 100%.

My name appeared in 75 responses, yielding 68.2%. The next most frequently mentioned people were Vasily Zharkov, Vladimir Nazarov, Dmitry Sevalnev and Alexander Trigub. However, a BMR occurrence may sit inside a quotation, a third-party list, a comparison or even a critical remark. Presence alone does not prove that the AI assistant endorsed the person as a contractor.

FRS: the position of the first recognized name

The second metric, First Recommendation Share (FRS), has a precise operational definition in this report: the percentage of answers in which a person's recognized full name is the earliest full person name in the text. At most one person is assigned that position per answer. A quoted third-party ranking, a historical remark or a critical passage is included if its name comes first. This rule was established after reviewing the collected material and then applied to the entire dataset.

Formula: FRS = answers where the name occurs before other recognized full names / 110 × 100%.

My name came first in 58 responses (52.7%). Zharkov led 17 (15.5%), and Nazarov led 5 (4.5%). Nine answers contained no recognized full person name. Of the 74 people, 21 appeared first at least once; the other 53 had an FRS of zero despite appearing somewhere in an answer.

“Recommendation” is retained in the historical metric name, but FRS is not a sentiment or endorsement score. It does not establish whether a user would read, trust, click or hire the first person mentioned.

The last column is my secondary calculation: first-name occurrences divided by all responses mentioning the same person. It is a way to describe this dataset, not a third independent measurement or a professional-quality score.

Consider Maxim Melnikov, who was mentioned in 19 answers but was never first. Sergey Sivkov appeared in just 9 answers yet was first in 2. A single ranking position would obscure the distinction between broad presence and the opening position in an answer.

For brand visibility analysis, I therefore separate mention frequency, position in the text, and the meaning of the passage. Whether the assistant was genuinely recommending a service provider still needs to be checked in context.

In ChatGPT, Perplexity and DeepSeek, my name appeared in all 10 responses. It was first in 9, 9 and 6 respectively. The two Alice interfaces gave a different result: Zharkov had both more mentions and more first-name appearances. He was also first more often in GigaChat. Leading the aggregate sample does not mean leading every interface.

These rows should not be interpreted as a controlled performance comparison between AI models. The geographic wording was not identical for every interface, and the dataset contains only a single response to each question. For a client project, I would design a consistent prompt panel and select platforms according to the actual customer audience before trying to explain differences.

How prompt selection affects visibility

The reports examined two segments of the same answers. In the relatively broad Q01–Q07 group, my name appeared in 50 of 77 answers (64.9%). In Q08–Q10, which emphasized personally managed projects, full-service delivery and case evidence, it appeared in 25 of 33 (75.8%). That is a difference of 10.9 percentage points. For Zharkov, the respective values were 37/77 (48.1%) and 8/33 (24.2%).

The groups were analyzed after data collection and differ on multiple dimensions. This comparison cannot establish that any one word caused the change. It does illustrate a measurement risk: a panel focused on tasks that match a particular consultant's messaging may produce more favorable visibility figures than a broad sample.

In practical GEO work, my recommendation is to agree on business-relevant questions before measurement, preserve their exact wording, and disclose who selected them. When the next measurement is made, use the same core questions. Treat newly introduced prompts as an additional series rather than quietly folding them into a claimed trend.

What the studies do not establish

The reports measure text occurrences and their order. They do not validate the professional credentials of every named person, compare client outcomes, estimate conversion, or prove why an AI assistant selected a given source. Existing lists and articles about participants could have influenced the responses. Name matching relies on a published dictionary and shortened name patterns; that process can misidentify people in rare cases.

The work was commissioned by an interested party, the counting rules were finalized retrospectively, some interfaces received altered prompt wording, and responses were not repeatedly sampled. Those details are material. On my own website, a visibility observation about me must not be presented as a third-party award or as proof that my services outperform all alternatives.

What I take into my clients' GEO measurement plans

Five rules carry over directly from this example.

  1. Preserve the observation. Record the prompt, interface, timestamp, response text and identifier. An impressive screenshot is an illustration, not a measurement series.
  2. Use distinct metrics. Brand presence, first appearance, sentiment, an actual recommendation and source citations describe different properties of a response. BMR and FRS cover only some of them.
  3. Keep a stable comparison panel. Repeated measurements should use the same business questions. Changes to the panel or the participating interfaces must be disclosed.
  4. Verify source facts. If an assistant names a provider, check the passage, the provider's actual specialization and any cited evidence. A frequent name can still accompany incorrect claims.
  5. Separate visibility from business outcomes. Track inquiries and sales alongside AI visibility where possible, but do not infer causation from a rising mention count alone.

This approach also informs my earlier analysis of sources used in GEO expert answers and my personal-brand GEO methodology. My professional biography provides the background and services I publicly claim; those statements should be assessed alongside external evidence, not accepted solely because they appear on GAEO.ru.

Conclusion: a measurable result, with clear boundaries

For the October 30, 2026 panel, my BMR was 68.2% and my FRS was 52.7%, the highest values among the 74 recognized names. That statement is limited to these 110 responses. It is neither an independent industry award nor evidence of superiority in professional skill.

The practical lesson is that AI-generated answers reveal different patterns when we measure presence, position and the meaning of a name in context. I use this distinction in the measurement process at GAEO.ru, including evaluations for businesses that want to understand whether and how AI assistants recommend their brands.
